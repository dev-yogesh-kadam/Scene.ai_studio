# Montage / time passing / process
Use when time is compressed: training, a relationship or a life in seconds, building or cooking or preparing, a journey. Mix with `grief` for a life that ends, `triumph` for training that pays off, `romance` for a love that grows or drifts.

## What the scene is really about
- Not time passing: someone changing. Name the start state and the end state before writing it ("clumsy → fluent", "close → strangers", "raw → ready").
- A montage is a scene with a turn. It needs a direction, a setback that charges it, and a moment that breaks the pattern.
- Each fragment is one telling detail of this person, not a generic activity (deleting one photo, not crying; re-tying a knot with taped fingers, not "training hard").

## Pick the engine
- **Same place, changing state:** one place returned to again and again; what changes in it (distance between two people, clutter, light, season, bodies) is the story.
- **One gesture across time:** a single action repeated, done differently each time (faster, gentler, by someone else, not at all).
- **Process in hands:** tools and materials up close in a rising rhythm, then the result with the maker in it.
- **Against a measure:** the same obstacle attempted early and failed, attempted again at the end (the stairs, the weight, the recipe, the jump).
- **Two threads, one rhythm:** two actions told in turns so each comments on the other (a ritual against a crime, a celebration against a loss).
- **The road:** one traveller held constant while the world changes around them, joined by matched frames or one continuous move.

## Staging and blocking
- Keep one thing constant (the place, the person's position) so change can be read against it.
- Make each state readable at a glance: posture, distance, light, wardrobe wear, the object's progress. If a fragment needs explaining, drop it.
- Stage the relationship in distance: the gap between two bodies at the same table or bench grows or shrinks across the run.
- Escalate the task: early fragments small and failing, later ones bigger and assured.
- Let other people mark the change: a coach who stops correcting, a partner who stops looking up, a child who now does the gesture.

## Performance
- Show skill in the body: hesitation, then economy. The last fragment should look effortless next to the first.
- One honest reaction per fragment (a wince, a grin at the mess, a glance that isn't returned), never a list of tics.
- Let fatigue and failure show at least once; a montage of only success has no turn.

## The turn
- The one time the pattern breaks: the gesture not answered, the place empty, the attempt that fails badly, or the first one that finally works.
- Put the opposite before it: a run of easy wins before the setback, a run of failure before the success.
- The setback can come from someone else (the coach leaves, the partner's chair is empty), not only from the task.

## After the turn / the end
- Drop out of montage time into real time: one longer moment at normal speed on the person, now changed.
- End on what the change opens onto (the fight they are ready for, the house now empty, the road ahead), with the person there.
- Leave the last fragment slightly open; not every repeated beat needs a tidy final echo.

## Traps
- Generic activities (jogging, laughing, clinking glasses) instead of acts only this person would do.
- The same motif three times, identical, ending as a neat echo; vary it or let it go unanswered.
- No setback: a straight line from start to finish reverses nothing.
- Every moment the same length, so no rhythm builds and none breaks.
- Ending on the finished object, the trophy or the empty room instead of the person who changed.
- A clever device (a mirror, a trick) carrying the change instead of plain behaviour.
- Too many places and people, so nothing is constant and nothing reads as change.
