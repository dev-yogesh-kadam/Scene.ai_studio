# Triumph / joy
Use when the feeling rises: a win, a breakthrough, a celebration, a training run paying off, a joyful reunion. Mix with `fight` or `chase` for the event being won, `romance` for a reunion, `comedy` when the win is lucky.

## What the scene is really about
- The distance travelled. Joy lands only against what it cost: the earlier failure, the doubt, the weight carried.
- Release. The body lets go of something it was holding (breath, shame, the role it had to play).
- Being seen. A win usually needs a witness: the crowd, the rival, the one person who doubted or believed.

## Pick the engine
- **Failure repaid:** an earlier failed attempt, same place and frame, now gets through.
- **The climb:** sustained effort as one travelling take that arrives somewhere, and the view opens.
- **The witness:** the win plays first on the faces watching it (the coach, the parent, the rival), then on the winner.
- **It spreads:** one person's joy catches the next until the whole frame is moving.
- **Quiet triumph:** no cheering; one private act says they made it (a long exhale, a laugh alone, sitting down at last).
- **Joyful reunion:** two people close a distance at full speed: the run, the collision, the hold.

## Staging and blocking
- Show the cost first, in one image: the strain, the bad attempt, the empty seats, the rival ahead.
- Bring the winner toward the lens; a figure coming at you is stronger than one going away. Keep the face visible for the payoff.
- Stage the win where the frame can open: the top of the steps, the finish line, the door into daylight.
- Height and space are the language: arms up, lifted onto shoulders, standing on something, the body growing in frame.
- Put the witness in depth beside or behind the winner, so the win and what it means share one frame.
- Give joy room: clear space around the body for the jump, the spin, the run.

## Camera and cutting
- Travel with the effort: one moving take for the run or climb, closing in as they near the end (full figure to head-and-shoulders), as in one famous stair run.
- Low angle at the win: they are bigger now. A rising camera when the feeling lifts.
- Shorten the cuts through the effort, then one long held shot for the win. The broken rhythm is the release.
- Hold wide enough to see the whole body celebrate; joy lives in the whole body, not the face alone.
- Slight slow motion only for the instant their sense of time breaks, if at all.
- Any music enters at the release, not before.

## Performance
- Fatigue before the win: heavy breath, a stumble, the last push. Exhaustion makes it real.
- Joy is physical and uncontrolled: a hop, a wordless shout, tears and laughter at once, hands on the head, a drop to the knees.
- Let the release overshoot: they don't know what to do with their hands; they hug the wrong person.
- Witnesses react in character: the stoic coach allows one nod, the mother covers her mouth.
- A reunion hug lasts longer than is comfortable: faces pressed, eyes shut, rocking.

## The turn
- The moment it is won: the line crossed, the top reached, the door opening on the face they wanted. Put doubt or near-failure just before it.
- Show the win directly, at full size, on the face. Never hide it behind a back or a crowd.
- It can come from someone else: the rival concedes, the crowd rises, one stranger starts clapping.

## After the turn / the end
- End on what the win opens onto: the view earned, the crowd that joins in, the person who stays.
- One beat of after (the body coming down, the first breath, a look to someone), then out.
- A triumph can carry a shadow (the cost, someone missing), told in one image inside the last shot, not a coda.

## For the video model
- Celebrations render: arms up, a jump, a spin, a run at the lens, confetti, light breaking. Keep one or two main bodies.
- Pile-ons, group hugs and tangled embraces warp; keep the crowd as a background moving mass and the hug to two people, slow.
- A run or climb is one travelling take at a steady pace, with stated start and end states (bottom of the steps → arms up at the top, city behind).
- Name the face plainly: mouth open in a shout, wet eyes, a grin showing teeth. Never "triumphant".
- Scoreboards, medals and written results garble; carry the win in bodies and space.
- Light rising (sunrise, a door opening on daylight, sky widening behind them) renders well as the visual lift.

## Traps
- Shooting the triumph from behind, hiding the payoff face by habit.
- Ending on a lone figure shrinking in a wide when the turn opens onto a view or a crowd; the view earned left offscreen.
- Scaling the payoff down: a polite smile where the scene earned a scream.
- No cost before the joy, so nothing reverses.
- Cutting the climb into pieces; the continuity is the effort.
- The trophy, medal or flag as the last frame instead of the person.
- Slow motion and swell across the whole clip, so the release has nowhere to go.
