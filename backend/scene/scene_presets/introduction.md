# Introduction / opening / first sight
Use when a story opens, a world is established, or a character (hero, villain, love interest) appears for the first time. Mix with `suspense` for a villain, `romance` for a love interest, `comedy` for an undercut hero, `reveal` for a delayed entrance.

## What the scene is really about
- A promise. The first image tells the audience what kind of story this is, what the world allows, and who matters in it.
- A character is introduced by what they do, not what they wear. Find the one act that holds their contradiction (the killer who is gentle with a cat, the hero who is out of his depth).
- The audience decides who someone is in seconds and rarely changes its mind. Make that first read deliberate.

## Pick the engine
- **Caught in the act:** we meet them mid-task, doing the thing that defines them, under a small pressure.
- **Delayed entrance:** others talk about them, react to them, clear a path; hands, back or silhouette first, then the face, given fully.
- **Through the room:** their status read from what everyone else does when they arrive (voices drop, someone stands, a chair is pulled out).
- **Emerging:** a shape far off that comes toward the lens out of haze, smoke, dark or a crowd until it becomes a person.
- **The undercut:** a grand image of them, then one move that deflates it (the pull-back that shows the heroic stance is on a sinking boat).
- **World first:** the place in one image that carries its rule (scarcity, danger, order, decay), then find the person inside it.

## Staging and blocking
- Give them something to do with their hands and something in their way. Character shows in how they handle an obstacle.
- Status in blocking: who is still while others move, who faces the door, who is lit, who takes the centre without asking.
- A villain is often the calmest person in the room and interested in something trivial. A hero often starts at a disadvantage.
- A love interest is introduced through the lead's eyes: the lead's look first, then what they see, then the lead's reaction.
- Stage entrances in depth, toward the lens; a figure coming at us lands harder than one walking away.
- For a world, put a person in the frame early (small in the wide, or the wide found from them) so the place has stakes.

## Camera and cutting
- First shot size is a statement: a wide says the world shapes this person; a close says this person will shape the world.
- A slow push-in that arrives on the face marks a star entrance. Save the arrival for the moment they do the defining act.
- Long lens for someone emerging from distance or watched without knowing; wide lens close for someone who owns the space.
- Angle only for a reason: low for menace or awe, level for an ordinary person we are meant to stand beside.
- If the face is withheld, the delay must pay: give it full size, lit, coming toward us. Never hide it by habit.
- Opening a world: one move that travels from the world's rule to the person is stronger than a stack of establishing shots.

## Performance
- One specific behaviour no one else would do: what they notice first, how they hold a cup, how they treat someone beneath them.
- Let the contradiction show in one act, not a list of traits: polite and threatening, cocky and scared, kind and hard.
- No posing. A character performing their own introduction reads as costume; a character busy with their own problem reads as a person.
- Others in frame stay alive and react; their reactions size the newcomer.

## The turn
- The moment the first read flips or deepens: the banter that ends in a drawn gun, the brute who is tender, the hero who fails at the easy thing.
- Put the opposite read first so the act reverses it (calm before the violence, swagger before the slip).
- It can be the world's turn too: the peaceful opening broken by the thing the story will be about.

## After the turn / the end
- One consequence beat, then send them into the story: they leave, the next problem arrives, someone follows.
- End on the person, now known, moving into what comes next. Not a pose, not a logo-like hero frame held too long.

## For the video model
- The opening frame sets identity. Give the character a distinct silhouette, wardrobe and colour, and pass a reference image every shot.
- Figures emerging from haze, smoke, backlight or darkness render well; a tiny distant figure loses identity, so finish the approach at a readable size.
- A slow push-in or a single pull-back reveal renders cleanly; whip pans and fast multi-cut reveals warp.
- Hiding the face, then revealing it in the same clip, can change the face. Reveal with a reference, and keep the hidden shot short.
- Signs, place names and title text garble; establish a city or era through architecture, light, vehicles and clothes.
- Crowds that react together (heads turning, a path opening) read; crowds doing individual business smear. Keep reactors few and near.

## Traps
- A wardrobe-and-pose hero shot, often slow motion, with no act; a stock "cool walk" instead of a gesture this person would make.
- Establishing shots of skyline or landscape with no person and no rule of the world in them.
- Telling who they are in dialogue that the first image should have shown.
- A delayed entrance whose payoff is small, dark or cut away from.
- A clever device (a reflection, a framed photo, a name on a door) doing the introducing instead of behaviour.
- Opening on an object insert that never leads to a person; ending on one.
- Introducing several people at equal weight so no one is introduced.
