# Performance / audience / the big moment
Use when a character performs in front of others: a song, a dance, a speech, a show, an audition, a battle, a sports moment watched by a crowd. Mix with `triumph` for a winning moment, `romance` when one watcher matters most, `confrontation` for a duel of performers, `comedy` for one that goes wrong.

## What the scene is really about
- Not the content of the act: the risk. Will they be seen, believed, accepted, or exposed?
- The scene lives in the line between the performer and the people watching. Decide which side we stand on.
- Usually one watcher matters more than the crowd (a parent, a rival, a lover, a coach, a judge). The performance is aimed at them, whether the performer knows it or not.

## Pick the engine
- **From the stage:** stay with the performer, looking out; the audience is lights and shapes, and we feel the fear turn into command.
- **The one watcher:** the act is seen through what it does to a single person in the audience.
- **The room turns:** a cold or hostile crowd won over, or lost, one person at a time.
- **Duel:** two performers or a performer and a judge; the cutting between them is the point of view, and the room swings with each round.
- **It goes wrong:** a frozen line, a fall, a dropped note, and the scene is what they do next.
- **No audience:** performing alone (a mirror, an empty hall), and one unexpected witness finds them.

## Staging and blocking
- Set the geography first: stage, crowd, the key watcher, the exits. Give the performer a clear eyeline to that watcher.
- Start smaller than the end: stiff hands, a shaky first phrase, eyes searching for a face. Growth needs a low start.
- Stage the crowd in depth: a few lit faces close, the mass behind as shape and light.
- Let the space change: the performer moves to the edge of the stage, steps off it, or the crowd closes in.
- Sports: show the play and its result in one clear frame, then the watchers.

## Camera and cutting
- Inside the act, not at a spectator's distance: on the stage, in the ring, on the field.
- Dance and physical feats: whole body in frame, few cuts, camera travelling with the performer so the move stays unbroken.
- Song and speech: close on the face and breath; the microphone, podium or instrument can be an obstacle in the foreground.
- Hold long takes while the performer struggles; let cuts quicken as they take command.
- Cut to the audience only for the watcher who matters, or to show the room turning. A random reaction at the peak breaks the spell.
- At the peak, come toward the performer's face, or let the camera leave the ground with them; don't drift off to the wide.

## Performance
- Nerves in the body before any note: breath, a hand that won't settle, eyes on the floor.
- The shift: the moment they stop performing for the crowd and do it for themselves or for one person. Show it in the eyes and shoulders.
- Watchers are alive and specific: a mouthed word, a hand to the mouth, a rival who stops chewing, a parent who doesn't clap.
- The performer's style is theirs: a move, a phrasing, a habit we have seen before, not a stock stage gesture.

## The turn
- The room flips (silence to roar, or roar to silence), the performer catches the key watcher's eye, or the act breaks.
- Put the opposite first: dread before the triumph, swagger before the collapse.
- The turn can come from the audience: a heckle, a walkout, one person who starts clapping, a judge who stands.

## After the turn / the end
- The beat after the last note: silence before the applause, and the performer's face hearing it.
- The look between performer and the watcher who mattered, given in full, both faces.
- End on the person and what the moment opens onto (the next stage, the cost, the relationship changed), not only the ovation wide.
- Sound: name the song or rhythm, and choose where it drops out or swells at the turn.

## For the video model
- Lip-sync on singing drifts. Play sung lines in profile, backlit, wide, or from behind; hold the face close on breaths, pauses and held notes. Use a lip-sync pass for anything frontal.
- Fingers on strings and keys warp. Show the body and face playing, not the fingering.
- Slow, weighted dance renders; fast footwork and tight ensemble sync tangle. One dancer reads cleaner than a troupe.
- Large crowds smear. Use haze, stage light, silhouettes and shallow focus, with two or three lit faces up front.
- Scoreboards, banners and signs garble; read the score from the people.
- Ball and contact physics glitch; show the strike and the outcome, then the reaction.
- One continuous take per musical phrase holds rhythm better than many short cuts.

## Traps
- Cutting to an anonymous face in the crowd at the emotional peak.
- Stock moves instead of this person's own: the fist pump, arms raised in slow motion, the mic drop.
- No stakes: a music video where nothing can go wrong and nothing changes.
- Hiding the performer's face at the payoff; it should build toward the lens.
- A crowd reacting as one identical mass, or the same reaction shot repeated three times.
- A private symbolic turn (a reflection, a memory flash) instead of the room or the watcher acting.
- Scaling it down: a performance that neither fails nor soars.
- Ending on the trophy, the empty stage or the applause instead of the person.
