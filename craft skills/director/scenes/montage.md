# Montage / time passing / process
Use when time is compressed: training, a relationship or a life in seconds, building or cooking or preparing, a journey. Mix with `grief` for a life that ends, `triumph` for training that pays off, `romance` for a love that grows or drifts.

## What the scene is really about
- Not time passing: someone changing. Name the start state and the end state before any shot ("clumsy → fluent", "close → strangers", "raw → ready").
- A montage is a scene with a turn. It needs a direction, a setback that charges it, and a moment that breaks the pattern.
- Each fragment is one telling detail of this person, not a generic activity (deleting one photo, not crying; re-tying a knot with taped fingers, not "training hard").

## Pick the engine
- **Same frame, changing state:** one locked setup returned to again and again; what changes in it (distance between two people, clutter, light, season, bodies) is the story.
- **One gesture across time:** a single action repeated, done differently each time (faster, gentler, by someone else, not at all).
- **Process in hands:** close, crisp inserts of tools and materials in a rising rhythm, then one wide of the result with the maker in it.
- **Against a measure:** the same obstacle attempted early and failed, attempted again at the end (the stairs, the weight, the recipe, the jump).
- **Two threads, one rhythm:** cross-cut two actions at matching sizes so each comments on the other (a ritual against a crime, a celebration against a loss).
- **The road:** one traveller held constant while the world changes around them, joined by matched frames or one continuous move.

## Staging and blocking
- Keep one thing constant (the place, the frame, the person's position) so change can be read against it.
- Make each state readable at a glance: posture, distance, light, wardrobe wear, the object's progress. If a fragment needs explaining, cut it.
- Stage the relationship in distance: the gap between two bodies at the same table or bench grows or shrinks across the run.
- Escalate the task: early fragments small and failing, later ones bigger and assured.
- Let other people mark the change: a coach who stops correcting, a partner who stops looking up, a child who now does the gesture.

## Camera and cutting
- The camera moves when life moves and falls still when it stalls; a still frame among travelling ones is the loss.
- Shots shorten as momentum builds. The run of cuts counts as one beat; then break it with one longer shot at normal speed.
- Bridge time on a match: a gesture, a shape or a framing that carries from one year into the next.
- Keep sizes consistent within a thread so the change, not the coverage, is what the eye notices.
- Dissolves for drift and memory; hard cuts for work and momentum.

## Performance
- Show skill in the body: hesitation, then economy. The last fragment should look effortless next to the first.
- One honest reaction per fragment (a wince, a grin at the mess, a glance that isn't returned), never a list of tics.
- Let fatigue and failure show at least once; a montage of only success has no turn.

## The turn
- The one time the pattern breaks: the gesture not answered, the frame empty, the attempt that fails badly, or the first one that finally works.
- Put the opposite before it: a run of easy wins before the setback, a run of failure before the success.
- The setback can come from someone else (the coach leaves, the partner's chair is empty), not only from the task.

## After the turn / the end
- Drop out of montage time into real time: one longer shot at normal speed on the person, now changed.
- End on what the change opens onto (the fight they are ready for, the house now empty, the road ahead), with the person in frame.
- Leave the last fragment slightly open; not every repeated beat needs a tidy final echo.

## For the video model
- Many locations in one generation warp and drift identity. Build the montage from separate short clips (2–5s each), one state per clip, same character references throughout.
- Fewer places, more states: one location with changed light, season, props and wardrobe renders far more consistently than eight places.
- Ageing and weight change need a reference image per stage; don't ask one clip to age someone.
- Calendars, clocks, captions and signs render as garbled text. Show time in light, weather, growth, wear and bodies.
- Pick the slow, readable moment of each action (the lift held, the dough folded), not the fast one.
- Time-lapse in a locked frame (light sweeping across, shadows wheeling) renders well and joins fragments.
- Sound: one line at most. Name the track or rhythm the cuts sit on, and where it drops out at the turn.

## Traps
- Generic activities (jogging, laughing, clinking glasses) instead of acts only this person would do.
- The same motif three times, identical, ending as a neat echo; vary it or let it go unanswered.
- No setback: a straight line from start to finish reverses nothing.
- Every shot locked, or every shot equal length, so no rhythm builds and none breaks.
- Ending on the finished object, the trophy or the empty room instead of the person who changed.
- A clever framing device (a lens covered, a mirror) carrying the change instead of plain behaviour.
- Too many places and people, so nothing is constant and nothing reads as change.
