# Backstory / lore / exposition
Use when the narration or a character states how things stand or stood: the family's history, who owns what, the contract's terms, the world's rules, the sect's ranks, how the curse began. Nothing changes in the present; the audience has to learn it. Mix with `montage` for a span of time, `flashback` when the past is one moment, `status` for power shown, `introduction` for a world's opening.

## What the scene is really about
- Information turned into pictures. Every fact needs a thing to see.
- Exposition is watchable only when it shows what is at stake for the hero: why this fact hurts them, or what it promises.
- A rule of the world is shown once, clearly; the story then plays against it.

## Pick the engine
- **The fact as action:** each stated fact shown happening (the family's rise as one ground-breaking, the war as one burning field).
- **The object:** one artifact carries the history (a portrait, a ring, a contract, a sword) and the telling keeps returning to it.
- **The map:** the place laid out in one move (the estate, the city, the sect's mountain), each part arriving as the narration names it.
- **Under pressure:** a character tells it in a hurry, or to someone who doesn't want to hear, while doing something else.
- **The rule shown:** the world's rule demonstrated once on a person (the power that burns its user, the contract that binds).
- **The gallery:** each person named, in one telling act.

## Staging and blocking
- Put the hero, or someone tied to them, in it wherever the line allows; lore with no person has no stakes.
- Show each fact at its true scale: an empire is told big and sweeping, a secret small and close.
- One image per fact; don't illustrate every word.
- Ground the world's rules in physical things: a mark on the skin, the colour of a robe, the size of a house.
- Use places, objects and people that return later; backstory is planting.

## Performance
- When people appear, they are doing something specific (signing, building, fighting, bowing), never posing.
- When a character tells it, the listener reacts; the telling is a scene with its own want.

## The turn
- The fact that matters most, the one the hero's story hangs on, gets the strongest image and the most room.
- Usually last: the history narrows to the present and ends on the hero.

## After the turn / the end
- Land in the present: the hero in the place, or holding the object, the backstory just explained.

## Traps
- A slideshow of still images with no motion.
- Picturing a figure of speech literally.
- Lore with no person in it, so nothing is at stake.
- Talking heads explaining what could be seen.
- New elements that never come back.
