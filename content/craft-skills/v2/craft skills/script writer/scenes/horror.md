# Horror
Use when the threat is something wrong with the world or the body: a creature, a ghost, an inhuman killer, possession, transformation. Mix with `suspense` for the wait before it, `chase` for the flight after it.

## What the scene is really about
- Horror is the violation of something safe: a home, a child, a body, a face you trust. Show the safe thing first so its breaking hurts.
- Dread is fear of what's coming, terror is the moment it arrives, horror is the look at what it left. Decide which this scene is mostly made of.
- The threat has a rule (it hunts by sound, it moves when unseen, it is inside her). Show the rule plainly once; the scene then plays against it.

## Pick the engine
- **Slow dread:** long still frames where nothing happens, then something in the background that wasn't there. The audience finds it before the character does.
- **Jump scare:** a rhythm built and broken. It lands early or late, never on the beat the audience counts.
- **Creature reveal:** a shape, a part, a shadow; then all of it, at the turn.
- **Body horror:** the body changes in stages, each worse, while the person keeps trying to act normal.
- **Wrongness:** an ordinary thing is slightly off (a smile held too long, a figure that never blinks). No scare at all; the unease is the scene.
- **Eruption:** warmth, a meal, a joke; then the horror bursts out in full view, and the scene is everyone's reaction.

## Staging and blocking
- Stage in depth: the character in front, the dark doorway behind. The threat appears far back, soft, still, before it ever moves.
- Empty space is a promise. Leave it where the threat will come from, and hold it.
- Show the safe place before the violation: the warm kitchen, the made bed, the family at the table.
- Let the character walk toward the dark, the door, the noise. Horror lives in going toward what you should flee.
- The victim acts: hides, fights, runs, bargains. A passive victim is a prop.
- Consider a surrogate: someone whose fear we see first, or who meets the threat first and doesn't come back.

## Performance
- Fear is physical: held breath, a hand over the mouth, eyes that won't look, a step back into a wall.
- People deny first ("it's the house settling"). Belief arriving is its own beat.
- The creature or the possessed: stillness and wrong timing beat thrashing. A head turning too slowly, a body standing up the wrong way.
- In body horror the horror is in the effort: the person keeps working, smiling, explaining, while the body fails.

## The turn
- The threat acts: it moves when it should be still, it's inside when it should be outside, it looks straight at us.
- Withhold the shape or the timing, never the event. When the scene is the creature or the body, show it fully at the turn.
- Let it all the way in: past the glass, through the door, into the light. An eye at the window is a beginning, not a payoff.
- One plain device at most; a step forward or a direct look needs no flourish.

## After the turn / the end
- One consequence beat: flight, a scream, a new rule learned, the thing gone when they turn back, the survivor's face.
- Horror endings may stay open: the thing still there, a small sign it isn't finished.
- Keep the aftermath short; a long hold on a body or the mess deflates fear into gore.

## Traps
- Hiding the creature or killer for the whole scene, or capping it at an eye, a shadow, a hand.
- Withholding the event instead of its cause, so the clip has no climax.
- The scare on the beat the audience expects.
- Stacked devices (a sudden light flip, a sting, a jump) on a moment that works plain.
- A passive victim.
- Gore held long after the scare.
- Invented rules, props or neat ironies the scene doesn't need.
- Revealing everything mid-clip, then idling.
