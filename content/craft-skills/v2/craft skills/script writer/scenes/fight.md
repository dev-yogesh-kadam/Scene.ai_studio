# Fight
Use for any physical clash: brawl, duel, ambush, one against many, a struggle over a weapon. Mix with `confrontation` for the build-up, `chase` when it travels, `horror` when one side is a creature.

## What the scene is really about
- A fight is an argument settled with bodies. Know what each side wants beyond winning (the door, the child, the gun, respect) and let the blows go after it.
- The story is who has the upper hand and how it changes. If nothing shifts, it is choreography, not a scene.
- Fighting shows character: the trained one is economical, the desperate one grabs anything, the tired one gets sloppy.

## Pick the engine
- **Brawl:** messy and close, with the upper hand changing 2–3 times; improvised from what the room holds.
- **Endurance:** one against many, or exhaustion. One unbroken sideways take; the blows get sloppier as he tires. No cleverness, just attrition.
- **Duel or standoff:** long stillness, then one decisive move. The beats quicken toward the draw: eyes, hands, faces, each moment shorter than the last. That run counts as one beat. Then the result, seen whole.
- **Ambush:** violence breaks into an ordinary moment with no warning; the scene is surviving the first seconds.
- **Struggle for one object:** a gun, a knife, a key between two bodies. Whoever holds it holds the scene.
- **Anticlimax:** the build promises a fight and the payoff skips it. Play it fast and don't explain the joke afterwards.

## Staging and blocking
- Set the geography first: who is where, the exits, the objects that will matter.
- Each exchange runs intent → action → reaction, cause and effect together.
- The hero starts at a disadvantage (outnumbered, unarmed, hurt, cornered) and uses the place: a door, a stair, a table, a pan.
- The weaker side strikes back at least once. Between equals, both draw blood.
- Fights travel: room to corridor, standing to floor, through a window. Each new space is a new beat.
- Let the space change meaning: the corner where the tender moment happened becomes where the violence happens.
- Show the pain. A wince, a limp, a hand shaken out makes the fighter someone to fear for.

## Performance
- Breath and fatigue build; by the end both fighters are slower than at the start.
- The reaction sells the hit more than the contact: the head snapping back, the stagger, the hand going to the wound.
- Faces between blows carry fear, rage, surprise. Give the loser a face, not just a body.
- Bystanders are alive: a flinch, a step back, a scream, someone who joins in.

## The turn
- The other side's move or mistake, seen clearly: a slip, an overreach, a dropped weapon, a hesitation, a third person stepping in.
- Put the opposite just before it: the winner sure of the kill, the loser almost finished.
- The decisive blow can land fully in view, or stay unseen and be read from what it leaves (a spray, a dropped blade, a late collapse). Choose; don't default to either.

## After the turn / the end
- The payoff plays to its physical end at full size: the body falls, the door gives, the house comes down.
- Then one consequence beat: the winner spent and shaking, a second attacker in the doorway, the witness who saw it all.
- Keep the aftermath short: one image of what is left (2–4s), then out. End on a person, not the dropped weapon.

## Traps
- Hiding the attacker for the whole fight.
- A one-sided beating between equals, or a hero who never strikes back.
- Scaling the payoff down (a near miss where the premise promised a fall or a kill).
- An endurance fight told in fragments, or a duel's peak drawn out.
- A clever environmental trick in place of plain effort and cost.
- A 6–8s aftermath that adds nothing, or a closing object held for seconds.
- Stopping at the blow without its consequence.
