# Conversation / everyday dialogue
Two or more people talking: kitchen, car, walk-and-talk, dinner table, phone call, exposition. The most common scene type. Mix with `romance`, `confrontation` or `comedy` once the talk takes a side.

## What the scene is really about
- Nobody talks to exchange information. Each person wants something from the other, and the lines are tactics.
- Find what the scene changes: a decision, a secret slipping out, a relationship tilting a few degrees. If nothing changes, cut the scene.
- The words are the surface; the scene lives in what people do while they talk and in how they listen.

## Pick the engine
- **Staged in depth:** one frame, actors move toward and away from the lens, so the shot changes size without cutting.
- **Walk-and-talk:** the camera travels with them; the destination is a clock, and arriving ends the scene.
- **The task:** they talk while doing something physical (cooking, packing, fixing a car); the task carries the subtext and stops when it lands.
- **Shot / reverse that tightens:** overs and singles, each pair a little closer, cutting on the listener.
- **The held two-shot:** no cuts, both faces readable, the audience chooses where to look.
- **One side heard:** a phone call, or the camera stays on the listener the whole time.

## Staging and blocking
- Start where the power is: who sits, who stands, who is on whose ground, who blocks the door.
- Give each beat a move: someone stands, crosses, turns away, sits back down. A new beat with no move reads as the same beat.
- Put the space to work: a counter between them, a doorway one keeps hovering in, a window one keeps looking out of.
- Dinner table: the seating chart is the drama. Seat for friction; the head of the table, who sits beside whom, the empty chair.
- Car: two people side by side who can't face each other; the road ahead is the future, the mirror is the past.
- Phone: the listener's space and business tell us who's on the other end before any name.
- Exposition: put it under pressure. Someone is in a hurry, someone resists hearing it, or the place itself keeps moving behind them.

## Camera and cutting
- Start objective (wide, longer lens, from a distance) and move closer as it heats up.
- Size follows importance: the wide for geography, the close-up for the line that changes the scene. Don't spend close-ups on small talk.
- Cut on the listener's reaction, not every speaker's first word. The reaction is where the scene happens.
- In a group, find the one or two people the scene is about and give them the singles; the rest live in two- and three-shots.
- When a bomb drops at a table, cut round the faces once, fast, then slow down and hold on the two who matter.
- Keep the 180° line clear at a table; cross it on purpose when an alliance flips.
- Walk-and-talks: lead them (camera retreating in front) for openness, follow from behind for pursuit, track alongside in profile for equals.

## Performance
- Play verbs: to charm, to stall, to fish, to warn, to reassure. Not "worried" or "casual".
- People interrupt, overlap, don't finish, answer a different question. Let a line hang unanswered.
- The listener is never neutral: a glance at the door, a slow nod, a hand that stops buttering bread.
- Business is character: how someone eats, pours, folds, fidgets. One habit per person, used when it matters.

## The turn
- Usually small: a line that lands wrong, a question not answered, a name dropped, a pause that runs too long.
- Mark it in the body: the task stops, someone sits down, the walk halts, the phone comes away from the ear.
- Before it, keep things easy (a joke, an ordinary rhythm), so the stop registers.

## After the turn / the end
- Enter late, leave early. Once the scene has changed, one beat of consequence and out.
- End on a reaction or an exit, rarely on the last line spoken.
- A conversation can end unresolved; one person left holding something the other just said.

## For the video model
- Lip-sync drifts on long lines. One or two short lines per shot; break a speech across shots or play part of it over the listener.
- Show one talking face at a time. Two or more mouths in frame often animate the wrong one; put the listener's back, shoulder or profile toward camera.
- Three or more speakers: carry them in singles and overs, not one wide where everyone talks.
- Slow walks with the camera retreating render well; brisk walks through crowds warp backgrounds.
- Screens and written messages garble; show the face reading, not the text.
- Sound: name the room tone and keep music out unless the scene needs it.

## Traps
- Subtext said aloud: characters explaining what they feel or what the audience already knows.
- Static coverage for its own sake: wide, over, over, close, close, with nobody moving.
- A designed device (a mirror, a symbolic object) to make talk "visual"; plain behaviour and a moving body do it better.
- Cutting to every speaker; the scene loses the listener.
- Every person in the room safe and still while one talks.
- Holding after the last line until the scene goes slack.
