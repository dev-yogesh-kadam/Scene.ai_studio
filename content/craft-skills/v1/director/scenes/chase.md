# Chase
Use for any pursuit: on foot, by car or bike, through a crowd or over rooftops, and the escape run from a collapsing place. Mix with `fight` when they catch up, `suspense` when the chase turns into hiding.

## What the scene is really about
- A chase runs on one quantity: the gap. Every beat makes it shrink or grow, and the audience must feel which.
- It is a run of choices under pressure: which turn, which door, who gets left behind. Character shows in those choices.
- Know what is lost if the gap closes, and what waits at the end (the border, the car, the train). Show that goal at least once.

## Pick the engine
- **Gap race:** pursuer and pursued share frames; the gap closes, opens, closes. Pure distance.
- **Escape run:** the place itself is the pursuer (a collapse, a flood, a fire, a rolling weight), coming head-on at the lens with the threat behind.
- **Hunted:** the pursued can't see the pursuer; running and hiding alternate, and every glance back is a question.
- **Hunter's view:** stay with the pursuer; we want the catch, or dread what he'll do with it.
- **Obstacle course:** each beat is one obstacle (a fence, a crowd, a dead end, a closing door) and what it costs to get past.
- **Slow chase:** stalled traffic, a crowd that won't part, a walk that can't become a run; the speed lives in the faces.

## Staging and blocking
- Map it first: where they are, where they're going, what is in between.
- Show the gap without words: the pursued passes a landmark, and the pursuer passes the same one a beat later.
- Two parties, one obstacle, one deadline. Extra vehicles, routes or cutaways turn tension into noise.
- Every beat costs something: a fall, a wrong turn, a crash, a lost shoe, a bystander knocked flat. Raise the cost each time.
- Let the terrain choose the beats: open ground plays the gap; corners, stairs and doors play loss and surprise.
- The pursued uses what the place holds (a slammed door, a pushed cart, a dive into the crowd); the pursuer adapts.
- The world reacts: pedestrians scatter, a driver brakes, a dog barks. An empty world makes the chase weightless.

## Camera and cutting
- A sustained run is one moving take that travels with it; cut when the place or the beat changes.
- Bring the runner at the lens. Coming at the camera reads as speed and danger; going away reads as escape or loss.
- Keep screen direction: both move the same way across the frame. Cross the line only when someone doubles back.
- Long lens compresses the gap and makes the pursuer loom; a wide lens close on the runner or inside the car puts us in it.
- Inside a vehicle, the windscreen and mirrors are the frame; the threat arrives through them.
- Long takes can break into fast cuts at the moment of flight; the shift itself is the jolt.
- When cuts come fast, keep the key action near frame centre so the eye never hunts.

## Performance
- Runners look back. Each glance is a beat: the pursuer closer, gone, somewhere unexpected.
- Fatigue builds: breath, stumbles, a hand on a wall, a stitch in the side.
- The pursuer has a face and a manner (relentless, calm, enjoying it). Faceless only when facelessness is the point.
- Panic makes mistakes; calm makes plans. Show which one is running.

## The turn
- The gap flips: the pursued doubles back, hits a dead end, finds the pursuer suddenly ahead, or his own plan backfires.
- Put the opposite just before it: the pursued sure he's lost him, the pursuer sure of the catch.
- Prefer the pursuer's move or the place's surprise over pure luck.

## After the turn / the end
- A chase doesn't end at rest. It ends in a catch, a crash, or an escape straight into the next trap (a locked door, a waiting rival, a drop).
- The escape plays to its physical end at full size: the door seals just behind him, the car clears the gap, the bridge goes down.
- One beat of breath at most, then the new problem or out.

## For the video model
- One runner, one direction, one camera move per shot. A take that travels alongside renders well; flailing sprint limbs and sharp direction changes warp.
- One obstacle per shot: one jump, one door, one vehicle.
- Car chases: two vehicles, a clear road, simple moves (swerve, overtake, near miss). Spins, pileups and tight weaving warp.
- Crowds as texture behind the runner, never tangled with him.
- Sell speed with the camera moving with them and things passing close to the lens, not with fast legs.
- Collapses and floods: one big readable mass moving one way, behind a runner who is small and clear.

## Traps
- No map: the audience never knows how close the pursuer is.
- Speed instead of stakes: fast cuts and engine roar with no cost per beat.
- A pursuer who is never seen.
- Cutting a sustained run into locked coverage.
- Ending at rest when the scene should open into the next trap.
- Scaling the payoff down (a near miss where the premise promised a crash or a leap).
- Invented gadgets for the escape instead of the place's own things.
- A long breather after the escape.
