# Grief / loss / bad news / mourning
News of a death, the moment it lands, the funeral, the empty days after. Mix with `reveal` when the news is the twist, `romance` for a farewell, `conversation` for the talk around it.

## What the scene is really about
- Not sadness: the gap between the world as it was a second ago and as it is now.
- The person keeps meeting the absence: a chair, a coat, a phone that won't ring.
- Grief is rarely one emotion. Numbness, anger, practical questions and a wrong laugh all belong.

## Pick the engine
- **The news arrives:** we learn it through behaviour before words (who comes, how they walk, what they take off).
- **Kept at a distance:** the worst moment played wide, through glass or out of earshot; the camera refuses the close-up out of respect.
- **The task:** grief inside a chore (dishes, paperwork, packing a room) that slowly stops working.
- **The space of the dead:** someone searches the dead person's room or things and finds the one that breaks them.
- **The delayed break:** composed through the public part, they break later, alone, over something small.
- **A whole life in seconds:** one repeated gesture across the years, changing each time, ending in absence.

## Staging and blocking
- Absence is the image: the empty chair, the missing body in a familiar frame, the second cup.
- Messengers keep moving toward the one who must hear; they don't freeze at a distance.
- Let the one who knows first be seen before the one who doesn't.
- Around a funeral or a wake, the living keep going in the background: food carried, chairs moved, a child playing.
- Give the grieving person somewhere to go: a doorway to lean on, a step to sit on, a sink to hold.

## Camera and cutting
- Static wides, the figure small, lots of empty space. Move the camera only when life moves.
- When life moves (a walk to the door, a search), travel with it; when it stops, the camera stops.
- Don't cut away at the moment it lands. Either hold the face, or go wide at the worst instant and stay there.
- Few cuts. A sudden cut to a close-up spends the moment; earn the one close shot.
- A death is shown as absence in the same frame, never as the act.

## Performance
- Size the behaviour to the news. Small loss: a task abandoned mid-motion. Devastating news: the body gives way (sways, sinks, folds), never a composed sit-down.
- Size it to the person too. Someone who never shows feeling breaks in the hands and breath (face into the cloth, silent shaking), not a fall.
- One honest reaction, played at full size, rather than a list of tics.
- Denial is an action: they keep doing the task, ask a practical question, correct a detail.
- The one delivering the news is grieving too; give them one thing they can't hide.

## The turn
- The moment the knowledge reaches the body, which is often not the moment it is spoken.
- Composure just before it (a steady voice, a joke, a tidy action) so the break reverses something.
- A second turn can follow: someone arrives, a message nobody expected, the comfort refused, then accepted.

## After the turn / the end
- Hold briefly. A long locked aftermath adds nothing.
- Grief can end in release (a reach, a step, a hand taken, light returning), not only stillness.
- End on the living person, not on a symbolic object left behind.

## For the video model
- Wet eyes, a breath that catches, a slow sink to the knees, a face pressed into cloth or a shoulder: these render well.
- Open-mouthed sobbing and wailing warp the face. Turn the face away, into a hand, or into another body.
- Fast collapses and falls warp; slow the fall and give the body something to hold on the way down.
- Hugs render when slow and still; faces buried in shoulders also hide warping.
- Keep mourners to a few clear figures; crowds drift. Show the dead as a hand, a covered shape or an empty frame, never a held face.
- Phone screens and letters garble; play the news on the face reading it.
- Sound: ambient only (room tone, a kettle, wind), so the model doesn't invent a sad score.

## Traps
- A composed reaction to catastrophic news; the body must answer the size of the news.
- A big collapse for a reticent person who would only go still.
- Scripted tics (a glance at the clock, a jaw that shakes) instead of one honest reaction.
- Feelings said aloud ("I can't believe he's gone").
- A stock gesture instead of one this person would make.
- Stillness as the default for every shot.
- The dead made to "answer" (a paw that slides off, the sun leaving on cue).
- Merging two turns into one, or dropping the second news that changes everything.
