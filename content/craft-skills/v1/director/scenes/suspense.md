# Suspense
Use when the threat is known or felt and the scene is the wait: a bomb under the table, a search, a guard who might look up, a secret about to come out. Mix with `confrontation` when the threat sits at the table; leave creatures and jump scares to `horror`.

## What the scene is really about
- Suspense is anticipation, not surprise: the audience knows something may happen and has to wait to see if it does.
- The question is simple and visible: will he find them, will she get out before he's back, will it go off.
- It lives in the gap between what the audience knows and what the character knows. Decide that gap before anything else.

## Pick the engine
- **The audience knows first:** show the threat before the character sees it, then let ordinary life go on in front of it.
- **The break-in:** an ordinary moment, then the threat breaks in with no warning; the scene is the next minute.
- **Shared ignorance:** we see only what he sees; alternate his look and his point of view so anyone or anything could be it. Let the wait run slow, but no more than half the clip.
- **The helpless witness:** someone watches from a distance (a window, a screen, a long lens) and can't intervene. The watcher's own act can give them away, and the watched looks back.
- **The ticking task:** a job to finish before time runs out (a lock, a search, a signature); every fumble is a beat.
- **The hide:** the character hides and the threat searches the same space. The question is how close it gets.

## Staging and blocking
- Show the clock in the world: a returning car, a guard's round, a kettle near the boil. The audience must know roughly how long is left.
- The threat can already be there and still: parked across the street, standing in the hall. Stillness frightens more than approach.
- Let the audience relax first: one or two false alarms, then tighten.
- Make an unseen threat visible through a proxy: something that reacts to it (a ripple, a swinging light, a trembling buckle).
- Leave empty doorways and frame edges where it could come from; hold longer than is comfortable.
- Keep the threat in behaviour; a shown weapon is the weakest reveal.
- Characters keep doing ordinary things; the scene plays the ordinary task against the danger.
- The one in danger acts: hides, stalls, lies, prepares. A passive target flattens it.

## Camera and cutting
- Static frames for being watched or trapped; a slow creep toward the one who doesn't know.
- A long lens compresses: cars, crowds and walls close in.
- Alternate threat, target and clock; each return is a little closer and a little shorter.
- Hold one take past comfort when the wait itself is the point; no cut means nothing will save them.
- Slow, then suddenly fast: when it breaks, the rhythm breaks with it.
- Sound, if it matters: strip to one small sound, or let one low pulse rise underneath.

## Performance
- Composure under pressure, cracked by small tells: a swallow, a hand stilled, eyes that refuse to go to the hiding place.
- The threat's manner: polite, bored, more interested in his food or his task than in the target.
- Bystanders who don't know keep the surface ordinary, and make it worse.

## The turn
- It comes from what the threat does, not only from what the target realises: a look straight into the camera, the threat filling the frame, the hiding place found, a question that shows he knew all along.
- Withhold the cause or the timing, never the event: when the scene is the eruption (an attack, a body), show it at the turn and end on the faces seeing it.
- Keep the threat a shape until the turn, then let it all the way in. The aggressor's face, held back until then, is often the strongest image.
- Don't stack devices on a moment that works plain; a direct look needs no dolly zoom.

## After the turn / the end
- Flight doesn't end at rest. Long takes break into fast cuts as he runs, and he lands in the next trap (a locked door, a waiting rival).
- Or the threat leaves and the target breaks only then: the panic held all scene finally comes out.
- If violence comes, imply it through fragments and reactions, never fast contact; a burst of short static shots can carry an attack the camera never fully shows.
- Afterwards, one move that ties it to what it means (an object, a place), not a long locked hold.

## For the video model
- Suspense renders well: stillness, slow moves, faces, small hand actions.
- One look or one small action per shot; let the sequence of cuts build the wait.
- A threat standing still in depth renders cleanly; a crowd of searchers doesn't.
- Clocks and tells as simple physical things large in frame (a door handle turning, a cup trembling), never text to be read.
- When it erupts, keep one clear action per shot.

## Traps
- Revealing the danger in shot 1, with no ordinary surface first.
- Defaulting to "the audience knows first" when shared ignorance or a break-in fits better.
- Hiding the aggressor for the whole scene.
- Menace that never breaks: polite all the way, with no eruption and no reveal.
- Too-neat ironies (the lock that sealed her in) and invented devices (a countdown chime) the place wouldn't hold.
- Revealing everything mid-clip, then idling.
- Ending the escape at rest.
- A held close-up as the stock turn.
