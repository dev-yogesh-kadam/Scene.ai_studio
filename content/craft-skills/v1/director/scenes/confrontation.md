# Confrontation / argument / interrogation / standoff / negotiation / threat
Two sides who want opposite things in one room. Mix with `suspense` for a hidden threat, `fight` once it turns physical, `conversation` for the quieter stretches.

## What the scene is really about
- Power. Who has it at the start, who has it at the end, and the exact moment it moves.
- Each side has a weapon: information, a person, a secret, patience, nothing to lose. Know both before shooting.
- Between intimates, the argument is never about its topic; it is about what the topic stands for.

## Pick the engine
- **Polite menace:** courtesy and small business, then it breaks, once, in frame.
- **Interrogation:** one questions, one resists, and power can flip to the one being questioned.
- **Equals:** two matched sides held in separate frames until one gives ground.
- **The row:** two people who love each other escalate; both draw blood; something gets said that can't be taken back.
- **Negotiation:** an object or a number on the table; the scene is who moves it, and who speaks first.
- **The standoff:** stillness, geography, then one decisive move.

## Staging and blocking
- Blocking is status: who sits, who stands, who owns the door, who is lit, whose ground it is.
- The table between them is a border. Crossing it, or walking round it, is an act.
- Menace lives in politeness and small business: a drink poured, food eaten slowly, a pen clicked. The stronger side can seem more interested in the food than in the other person.
- A row moves through rooms: one leaves, comes back, follows the other into the kitchen. Let walls and doorways cut them off.
- Three-way standoffs: geometry first (a high wide of the triangle), then each face in turn so the audience reads every calculation.

## Camera and cutting
- When one side is weaker, stay with them, and let the stronger one fill the frame.
- Between equals, the cutting between them is the point of view. Overs and singles, no shared frame, until one gives; the first two-shot shows who won.
- Shots tighten every beat. Lenses can grow longer and the camera lower as the walls close in.
- Cut on the listener, not the speaker; the line lands on the face it hits.
- Angle follows power; level while nobody wins.
- In an interrogation, show the flip by who controls the frame and the light.
- Standoffs: cuts get faster and closer toward the draw (eyes, hands, faces); that run counts as one beat. Then a single wide for the result.

## Performance
- Play verbs: to corner, to bait, to flatter, to stonewall, to wound. Never "angry".
- Menace is calm. The louder side is often the losing one.
- In a row, the cruel line comes quietly. Hold on the one who said it as well as the one who heard it.
- Let the weaker side hold together, then leak: a swallow, a hand that can't stay still, an answer too fast.

## The turn
- Prefer the other side's move: information used as a weapon, a sudden calm, an unexpected answer, a choice forced on the opponent.
- When polite menace breaks, it breaks in frame and close enough to feel: a slam, a thrown object, an object defiled (a cigarette stubbed in the food). It can break first on a bystander mid-scene, to crack the one being questioned.
- A hand on the other's body is the loudest break; save it for when nothing smaller will do.
- The weaker side strikes back at least once. When they lose, they answer with a physical tell (flee, freeze, spill, break down), not a counter-line.

## After the turn / the end
- One frame that shows the new order: who stands, who is in the light, who leaves.
- A spent winner, emptied by what he did, is often truer than a victory pose.
- Or don't settle: the loser bolts, finds the door locked, walks into the next trap.
- After a row, the silence and the distance between them is the ending; one beat, then out.

## For the video model
- Slaps, shoves, grabs and thrown punches warp. Show the wind-up and the result, or let the violence land on an object (a table, a glass, a door).
- Shouted lines distort mouths and lip-sync. Keep shouts to a few words; play the long speech calm.
- Two faces talking in one frame confuse lip-sync. Use overs with the listener's back to camera.
- Stillness, slow leans in, a slow stand: these render cleanly and read as threat.
- Small objects handled slowly (a glass, a pen, a coin) read well; fast hand business smears.
- Sound: let the room carry it (a fan, a fridge, traffic outside) and keep any score low until the break.

## Traps
- A private, symbolic turn (seeing his own reflection) instead of the opponent acting.
- Menace that never breaks, polite all the way to the end.
- Menace made physical by habit (a wrist grabbed) where an object or a silence would land harder.
- A weapon shown as the turn; keep the threat in behaviour and words.
- Forcing a single point of view onto equals.
- The loser answering with a clever line instead of the body.
- Subtext said aloud: both sides explaining what they want.
